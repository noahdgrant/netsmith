# A chain draws from the net it starts on

Amended by ADR 0026 and ADR 0027: the balance solves the current of a rail or a port, and a part's through-current only from a complete balance of two terms.

ADR 0007 summed only the currents of rails, so an LED and a resistor on a rail cost the supply nothing and a board had to say `.input.current == -.led.current` itself. A part's current is already in the model: `Resistor.current` is the current from `.a` to `.b` and `Led.current` the current from anode to cathode, and a `~>` chain runs through a part from the first terminal of its `bridge` to the second. The convention that makes this usable is that a part's `current` field, when it has one, is the current through its bridge in that direction.

A chain that starts on a net a rail draws from takes the through-current of its first part from that net: a positive term in the balance there, the way a rail that draws is positive. The same current comes back where the chain ends, so the through-current of the last part is a negative term at the end net. Each end reads its own part. A series chain passes one current, so the checker equates the currents of neighbouring parts in a chain (`r.current` and `led.current` in `rail.pos ~> r ~> led ~> rail.neg`) and a design that states two different currents for them is told so by the solver, with both. They are equated only where nothing else is on the net between the two parts: a third terminal there (a divider with a load on its middle) can take some of the current, and then nothing says the two are equal. A part that has no `current` is skipped. With this the board writes no draw of its own: `examples/two_boards.nsm` loses its `.input.current == -.led.current`, the LED board's input comes out as the negative of the LED's current, and through the join it reaches the regulator board.

What comes back must have a net to come back to, so a rail's `neg` pin is a term too: what a rail draws through `pos` comes back at `neg`, so there it counts with the opposite sign. A net with a rail on its `pos` is a supply net and is balanced and solved from as in ADR 0007. A net with a rail only on `neg` is a return net (ground), and its balance is checked and never solved from: its terms repeat those of the supply nets, and a range added a second time is wider than the ranges it came from, so solving from it would loosen what the supply nets already settled. A fault shows on both kinds of net; a return net is checked only when nothing has been reported, so it is said once. A return net with a single term is not balanced, because that is a rail whose `neg` goes nowhere in particular.

A chain is counted only where the supply is: if it starts on a signal (the enable line of the status indicator), what drives it is not modelled, and a half of it would not balance, so neither end is counted. A chain is whole when every part in it passes a current, no junction is tapped, and it ends on a net a rail is on. A chain that is not whole is drawn from its start if its first part has a current, and gives nothing back; the return nets of its scope are then not checked, because what comes back there is no longer all accounted for. Without this a divider with a load, or an LED that a logic pin pulls down, would be refused for a fault that is only the model being incomplete.

A capacitor draws no current at DC and has no `current`, so it adds nothing to a sum, and a chain through one counts only as far as its parts that pass a current. A part that draws without being in a chain, such as the supply pin of an IC, declares it the way a board declares a supply: an interface of the kind that carries a current, here a `Rail` (`.vdd: Rail`), whose `current` is what the part draws through `.vdd.pos`. It counts as any rail of a part does (the regulator is one), and needs nothing new. A pin that draws with no rail to hold the voltage is not modelled.

A failed requirement on a current says what the current is made of. When a regulator's `-.vout.current <= .max_current` fails, the message follows the current through the balances it is in and lists, for each net, the terms and their ranges, so a board with 90 mA of stated load and an LED branch of 7 to 17 mA is told it asks the regulator for up to 107 mA of 100 mA, and by which parts. The message for a net that does not add up now lists every term as well as the one that failed, and the rest of the net is called "the others on it" and not "the other rails", since they are no longer only rails.

The exception of ADR 0010 stays but means less: a board's own port that supplies the inside, with nothing inside that the checker knows takes current from it, is taken as stated and not reported. What remains unknown is a part that draws and is neither in a chain from a rail nor holds a rail of its own. A load with nothing to supply it is still reported.

Limits. A pin that draws with no rail, and the tap of a divider, are not modelled: a chain with a tap draws its start current and nothing is checked on the return. A capacitor in the middle of a chain with parts that pass a current makes the chain partial, and its DC current is not set to zero. The balance holds in the worst case for each current separately (ADR 0007): the terms of a net are independent ranges, so it is necessary and not sufficient. A part's quiescent current and a regulator's own are still ignored.
