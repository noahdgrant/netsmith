# An interface is drawn as one bus in the schematic

The editor's schematic drew each pin of an interface as a net of its own: a UART between a processor and a modem was four wires or, where the modem is on another sheet, four flags on the processor's sheet and four on the modem's. The language knows that the four are one `Uart`, and the schematic did not say so. It is now drawn as one **bus** (GLOSSARY): the pins stay one by one on the part's edge, each member's wire is a short entry that meets one thick line, and the line runs to the other part, or ends in one flag where the members go on to another sheet. The line is named once, by the interface instance (`uart0`), with the list of members under it (`{tx, rx, rts, cts}`) and a slash with their count.

**The data comes from the checker's model, not from net names.** `Design` has a new field `interfaces: Vec<InterfaceInstance>` (`netsmith_core`), each with the path of the instance (`mcu.uart0`), the interface's definition (`Uart`) and the members that are on nets, each with its name (`tx`) and the name of its net. Lowering reads it from the model's nets, which keep, besides the parts' terminals, the ports of modules and the pins of interfaces that only pass a connection along (`Net.pins`). An instance is listed for every `Interface` in the design: the port of a module (`mcu.uart0`) and the interface of the part inside it (`mcu.u.uart0`) are two instances of the same nets, and so are the two ends of a join on the board. Rails are instances too (`Rail`); the schematic draws them as power symbols and never as a bus. The field is additive: a design that does not know its interfaces (a test's small design) has none and is drawn as before. It is not written to any file, so nothing already written depends on it; it is a plain, small structure that the view reads, and widening it later (a direction, a role) breaks no one.

Why not read it from names: `UART0_TX` and `uart0_rx` say nothing the language did not say first, and a name that merely looks like a member (`SPI1_CS1` on a GPIO) would make a bus of what is not one. A bus is only drawn for what the source declared as one interface.

**The rules.** The drawing decides from the placed sheet (`crates/netsmith-view/src/layout/engine/bus.rs`):

1. A net is a candidate when it is not a rail, has a name that someone chose, and has exactly one pin on each of two main parts (an IC, a connector), or exactly one pin on one main part when it goes on to another sheet. A net with a part beside its pin (a series resistor, a pull-up, a protection diode) is not a candidate: its pin has a wire to that part, and the stub is the part's.
2. Per interface instance, the candidates with the same two parts and the same two sides of those parts are one bus when there are **three or more**. Fewer stay as they were (a flag or a name each, or a wire). A member whose pin is on another side of its part than the others' stays out: the trace-output pin of a debug port, on the far side of a processor, stays a flag beside the bus of the port's other pins.
3. A net is in the bus of one instance: the instance with most members, then one in the module of one of the parts (the module's own port, not another module's), then the shallowest path, then the path. The first end of a bus is the part in that instance's own module, else the first by path. The members are listed in the order of the pins at the first end, from the top or the left, so the entries do not cross; the other end's entries are in the pin order of that part, and the bus is named once, with a second line (`uart: {rx, tx, cts, rts}`) only when the other end's interface has another name or other member names (a modem's `tx` is the processor's `rx`).
4. The drawing is orthogonal. Each entry is a straight wire from its pin to the spine, a thick line beside the pins, in the first column past everything else at that side of the part (two cells past the stub of any other pin). The trunk, also thick, joins the two spines or runs from the spine to the flag, routed on the grid like any wire; it leaves the spine level with the other end where the two overlap. The count is a slash across the trunk and the number beside it. A bus that cannot be drawn (no room for the spine, no route, no place for its writing) falls back to the nets as they were: the check never sees a half-drawn bus.
5. The line is thicker than a wire (0.9 mm against 0.25 mm), the name and the list are written as net names are, and the SVG says it with classes (`bus`, `bus-entry`, `bus-label`, `bus-count`) and with `data-bus` (the instance's path) and `data-net`.

**The check treats a bus as no conductor.** The thick line joins nothing: the members' entries carry their nets and join, by name, the net of that name at the other end, as a label does. `netsmith_view::check` rebuilds the netlist that way, and the rule `bus-member` reports an entry whose net is not one of the bus's members, a member with no entry (or two) at an end, two members at one place of the line, an entry off the line, and a wire that touches the line away from an entry. The line is also checked as a wire against bodies, texts and the wires of other nets. `metrics` counts a bus once in crossings and bends, and counts buses and their members.

**Rejected.** Four wires side by side (the drawing before), which is what the owner asked to be rid of; a bus entry at 45 degrees, as KiCad draws it, because the drawing engine only draws horizontal and vertical wires and a comb of straight entries reads the same; one symbol pin for the whole interface (option A of the owner's comparison), which hides what is wired to which pin and takes the pins off the part; naming a bus by the first net's prefix.

**The cost.** A bus's writing and spine need room beyond the part: the part is placed with that room, so a sheet is a little wider where it has one (on the private test board of ADR 0048, the widest processor sheet with three buses grew by about a seventh). A bus that crosses a sheet boundary shows the instance's name at each end; the two sheets' names may differ (`uart0` on one, `uart` on the other), and the member lists differ in the same way, so the reader finds the other end by the member lists and the nets' names in the tooltips, not by one shared name. The editor follows a bus flag to the sheet at the other end as it follows a net's flag, matching on the member nets, not on the name: a click opens the sheet whose pins carry at least two of the nets (all of them for a bus of two), with the bus drawn there picked, and a menu asks where several sheets do. A bus is picked as its first member net, which the other views light, and the Schematic remembers the bus while the selection is still that net.

**Left open.** The threshold (three) and the rule that a part beside a pin stops its net from being a member are choices a person may want otherwise (a protection diode on each line of a USB pair is a bus in a datasheet). The direction of the members is not drawn (the language says it in the pin kinds of the parts, not in the interface). An interface that nests another (`Usb2` has a `Rail`) is drawn without the nested one. A bus whose members end on more than two parts (a shared I2C line) is not a bus here; it stays as nets.
