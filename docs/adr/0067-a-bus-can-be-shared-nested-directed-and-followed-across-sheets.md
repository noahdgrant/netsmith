# A bus can be shared, nested, directed and followed across sheets

ADR 0049 drew an interface as one **bus** (GLOSSARY) and left four things open: the direction of the members was not drawn, an interface that nests another was drawn without the nested one, a bus with more than two parts on its lines (a shared I2C or SPI bus) was not a bus, and a bus that goes on to another sheet said only its own name, so the reader of the other sheet had to find the way by the member lists. All four are drawing conventions of the schematic view (`crates/netsmith-view/src/layout/engine/bus.rs`). The language, the checker, the lowering and `Design` are not touched: the view reads the same `Design::interfaces` and the pin tables of the parts, as before.

## A flag says where the bus goes

The flag of a bus that leaves a sheet had one line of writing beside the thick line: the instance's name and the list of its members. It has a third line, `→ modem.uart: {rx, tx, cts, rts}`: the path of the instance at the other end, and what that instance calls the members, in the order this sheet lists them (the modem's `tx` is the processor's `rx`, so the lists differ). The instance is found as it was for the two-ended bus: another instance, of another module, with most of the same nets; for a flag it is also an instance that belongs to no part on this sheet (`sensor1.spi` of a part that is drawn here is never "the other sheet"). `Bus::other_path` has the path, the SVG puts it as `data-other` on the `bus-flag`, and a page that follows the flag has the instance to look for, not only a name to guess from. The editor already follows a flag by its member nets (ADR 0049) and is not changed.

I read "bus nets with different names on each side" as the two names an interface has on two sheets (`uart0` and `uart`, `tx` and `rx`): a net has one name in the model, so one electrical net cannot be named two ways. Two nets that a part joins (a level translator, an ESD array) are two nets and two buses, not one; a bus that follows a signal through such a part is not built (left open).

Measured on the test board `UartSheets` (two sheets, `main` at 2b24cf6 before): the sheets together are 231x141 mm before and 231x157 mm after (the trunk to the flag is longer, so that the third line has room along it); the length ratio 0.79 to 0.84; bends, crossings and every rule of `check` are the same (0 rules broken). Rejected: the other sheet's name (the engine draws one sheet and does not know what the others are called; the instance's first segment is the module, which names the sheet on a board drawn by module), and a list only when the names differ (a bus whose names are the same on both sides still needs to say which sheet it goes to).

## The direction of a member

The interface does not say who drives a member: `std/bus.nsm` says so itself, and ADR 0016 puts it in the pin kind of the part that has the bus. So the drawing reads the pin kind. Each entry whose pin is an `output` has an arrowhead pointing away from the part, an `input` one pointing at the part, a `bidirectional` pin two (one each way, a third and two thirds along the entry); a pin of any other kind (`power`, `passive`, `no_connect`), and a part that states no pin table, has none. The arrowheads are triangles 1.1 mm long and 1.0 mm wide on the entry's own wire (`BusEntry::arrows`), filled with the wire's colour; the SVG has `<polygon class="bus-arrow bus-arrow-out|in|inout" data-net="…">` in the bus's group and writes the look into the style only for a drawing that has one, so a drawing with none is byte for byte as it was. `BusEntry::flow` says which (`MemberFlow::Gives`, `Takes`, `Both`), `out` the way the wire runs from the pin to the thick line and `length` how long it is.

No arrow is drawn on the thick line: the members of one bus go both ways (a UART's `tx` and `rx`), so the line has no one direction, and the arrows at the entries say what a part does with each. The list of members is not marked either; the first end's entries are in its order and say it.

Rejected: reading the flow of the KiCad symbol's pin (the library's guess, not what the language says; a part with a symbol and a pin table that disagree would be drawn after the library); arrows in the member list (`tx→ rx←`: the list is in the first end's order and the second end's arrows would need a second list); an arrow on the line for a bus whose members all go one way (rare, and the entries say it).

If ADR 0064 (bus roles and levels) is accepted, a role (controller, target) is another thing a bus could say; this ADR does not wait for it and does not use it.

## A nested interface is one bus with dotted names

An interface instance that sits inside another listed instance (`mcu.link.data`, an `Spi`, inside `mcu.link`, a `Link` that also has `irq`) gives its members to the outer one under its path from it: `data.sclk`, `data.mosi`, `data.miso`, `irq`. The outer interface is one bus: it has the four members, the count 4 and the list `{data.sclk, data.mosi, irq, data.miso}` in the order of the first end's pins. Drawn as it was, `data` was a bus of three named `data` and `irq` a net with a flag or a name of its own, and the reader could not tell that they were one `Link`. A nested `Rail` is power and is not a member (the schematic draws rails as power symbols, ADR 0049). Measured on `UartNested` (the board of the UART with its four lines declared as a `Link` with an `Spi` inside it): one bus before with 3 members and a label at each end for `irq`, one bus after with 4 and no label; wires 20, bends 12, crossings 2 and the size 77x69 mm are the same; on `UartNestedSheets` the sheets together go from 231x145 to 231x174 mm (the flag's third line again). The inner instance stays a candidate of its own and is used only for nets the outer one does not take (a tie goes to the outer: more members, then the shallower path); the nets are counted for the threshold as one set. A sheet keeps the instances around one that has a member on it, even with none of their own, so that the nest has the same name on every sheet.

The lowering lists an instance only when it has members of its own on nets, so an interface that only nests others (`Bridge` with a `.a: Uart` and a `.b: Spi` and no pin of its own) has no instance, and its parts are two buses named by their own leaf as before. Listing such a container is a change to `netsmith-lower` (a few lines, and an instance with no members) that I did not make; the drawing is ready for it (the sheet filter keeps a container and `flat_members` reads one).

Rejected: a thick line for the outer interface with thinner lines branching off for the inner (the drawing engine has one weight of thick line, the entries would cross the branches, and the reader gains the same from the dotted names); one bus for each level (the drawing before); the inner name in braces (`{irq, data{sclk, mosi}}`: the members are listed in the order of the pins, so the groups would not be contiguous).

## A bus shared by several parts

A net is a candidate for a bus when every one of its pins on the sheet is a pin of a main part, with one pin on each of two or more parts (or one part when the net goes on to another sheet, as before), none hidden. The case that was left out is three or more parts, or two parts and the flag. Per interface instance, the candidates with the same set of parts, the same flag and the same side of each part are one bus when there are **three or more** members between two places (a part, or the flag), and **two or more** between three or more places: a shared I2C is `sda` and `scl` on three parts. At most eight parts are at the ends of one bus. A member whose pin is on another side of a part than the others' stays out, and so does a net with a part beside a pin (a pull-up, a series resistor): the rule of the two-ended bus is not changed.

The drawing is one thick line that is a tree. Each part has its entries and its spine as before; the first end is the part in the instance's own module, the others follow in the order of their paths. The trunk joins the spines of the first two ends, routed on the grid as before; each further spine runs to the nearest node of the line already drawn (`Grid::route` with the nodes of the line as its targets) and ends on it, where a branch of a bus meets a bus without a dot. When the members go on to another sheet the flag leaves the first spine straight out, as for one part, and the other ends are joined to the line the same way. The count, the name and the list are written once, along the longest run of the line with room. The check treats the thick line as before: no conductor; each entry joins the net of its name; the rules `bus-member` and the wire rules hold for any number of ends (`ends` is a list; it never had to be two).

Each part of the bus declares the same members, so the instances that could name the bus are all equal (the same count, each local to its part): the first by path names it (`mcu.spi` before `sensor1.spi`), which is a deterministic choice and not the controller. Naming the bus by the instance of the part whose pins are outputs on the clock would be a guess from the pin kinds, which I did not make.

Measured on the test boards of `crates/netsmith-view/tests/common/bus.rs`, before (`main` at 2b24cf6: each shared net is a name at every pin, no line) and after:

| board | buses | bends | crossings | wire length / half perimeters | size mm |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `SpiShared` (one processor, three sensors, three lines) | 0 → 1 (3 members, 4 ends) | 14 → 19 | 2 → 6 | 0.55 → 0.91 | 118x121 → 160x65 |
| `I2cShared` (two sensors, two lines) | 0 → 1 (2 members, 3 ends) | 10 → 15 | 2 → 5 | 0.84 → 0.91 | 118x60 → 118x62 |
| `SpiSharedSheets` (two sheets) | 0 → 2 (3 members each, 3 and 2 ends, one flag each) | 18 → 24 | 2 → 6 | 0.43 → 0.63 | 154x295 → 213x268 (the two sheets together) |

The bends and crossings go up. The cause is the supply: a pin of the power rail at the bottom of a part is wired to the rail at the top of the sheet, and that wire crosses the thick line, which runs across the sheet between the spines. Named stubs crossed nothing. A hand-drawn schematic crosses the same wires, and the owner chose the bus for the two-ended case knowing the same cost (ADR 0049); but it is a cost and the numbers say it. No rule of `check` is broken on any board, with or without rails told.

## Left open

*A pull-up on a shared bus.* An I2C bus always has pull-ups (ADR 0044), and a net with a pull-up beside a pin is not a member, so an I2C bus whose pull-ups are on the same sheet as its devices is still drawn as nets. When the pull-ups are in a module of their own, drawn by module (`I2cPullups`), the sheet with the devices has the nets continue (a flag) and is drawn as a bus. A pull-up as a branch off the thick line (a resistor whose signal pin ends on the line like an entry) is the way to close it; it needs the engine to place a small part beside a line and not beside a pin, which it does not do. *Pure containers* of interfaces (above). *The thresholds* (three members between two places, two among three, eight parts) and the rule that a part beside a pin keeps a net out. *Which instance names a shared bus* (above). *A bus through a part*: a level translator or an ESD array that joins two nets. *The direction as a role*, if ADR 0064 is accepted. *Whether the arrowheads should be a setting* (a very busy bus of bidirectional pins, a processor's GPIO, has two on each entry).
