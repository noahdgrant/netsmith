# A chain carries the peak of its rail to the parts in series

Extends ADR 0013 (a part across a rail has its voltage as `.bias`) and ADR 0014 (a chain draws from the net it starts on) to a part in series. The text before the cut is `git show 7679773:docs/adr/0086-a-chain-carries-the-peak-of-its-rail-to-the-parts-in-series.md`. Meets the second test: the checker gives a part a value because of the name of one of its fields, which the code of the part does not show.

A part reads its own fields. What reaches it from outside is its `.bias` (ADR 0013) and its `.current` (ADR 0014). A fuse in a chain from a rail is across no rail, so it saw the chain's current and nothing of the rail, and its `.rated_voltage` was compared with nothing. Every board with a fuse wrote `.fuse.rated_voltage >= .input.peak` itself, or had a fuse picked that was rated below the peak it sits behind (issue 175, a real 12 V automotive board). The rule that a part states its own requirements (`docs/DESIGN-RULES.md`) said the fuse should state this one, and it could not name the rail. The owner chose, on issue 175, to pass a value along a `~>` chain; this is the smallest reading of that.

A part that has a `bridge` and a voltage field named `.supply_peak` and is in a `~>` chain is given the `.peak` of a rail, as the relation `part.supply_peak == rail.peak`, made after the nets are known as the bias is. The rail is one whose `pos` is on the net the chain starts on, among the rails the part's scope can see: the rails the scope declares itself, then the rails of what it holds, and the first by path within each, so statement order does not matter. Every part of the chain gets the value of the start, the later parts too: it is an upper bound, since a part before them only drops voltage, and the drop is not subtracted. A chain that starts on a net with no such rail (a signal, a rail's `neg`, a part's pin) gives its parts nothing. The standard `Fuse` is the one definition that has it, and states `.supply_peak <= .rated_voltage`. A rail with no stated `.peak` leaves the check not evaluated; a fuse with no rail to carry it states `.fuse.supply_peak &= 24V`.

It is a convention of a field's name, not a keyword: the checker looks for `supply_peak` as it looks for `bias`, `current`, `voltage` and `pos`. ADR 0088 and 0091 (folded; their rules are in the guide and in the doc comments of `Diode`, `Tvs`, `Fuse` and `Rail`) use the same convention for four more names: `.bridge_voltage`, `.supply_reverse_peak`, `.limit_current` and `.supply_fault_current`.

Rejected, each a language or std-interface change: syntax that names the carrying (`.fuse: Fuse { .supply_peak ~ rail.peak }`), which adds a keyword and still has to find the rail; the rail by name (`.fuse.rail ~ .input`), which makes every board say the line this removes; all the rails on the start net and not the first, which is sound for rails whose peaks differ but leaves no single equation to read, and rails joined with `~` share the peak anyway; the same value as `.bias`, read as the drop across a series part, because one name would mean two things; a std module of the input protection with a fixed topology (fuse, diode, load), which does not replace the rule, since a board that builds the circuit by hand has the check too; `Switch` and `Inductor`, whose `.rated_voltage` nothing compares, one line each once this is accepted; `Tvs.protected_limit`, left as ADR 0023 has it.

Cost: a board with a fuse and a rail with no `.peak` has one more check listed as not evaluated, and the catalog can pick a different fuse for a board that states a peak. A newcomer will not find the rule by reading the fuse alone: the doc comment of `Fuse` and the guide say it.

Left open: whether a field name is the right form at all or the owner wants syntax (each new part that follows the convention makes a change dearer); a part in a chain through a module's port (the chain stops at the port, so a fuse in a module takes the peak of the port, which the `~` that joins it to the board's rail shares); a part that drops voltage before the fuse (a diode, a second fuse) is not subtracted, so the check is conservative by that drop; `Switch` and `Inductor` do not have the field.
